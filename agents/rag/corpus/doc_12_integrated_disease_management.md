# Integrated Disease Management for Solanaceous Crops

## Scope

This note covers practice common to tomato, potato and other solanaceous crops, independent of which disease has been identified. Chemical control is the last of four steps, not the first: field hygiene, scouting, and cultural adjustment together decide whether a spray programme works or merely delays a loss. A plan that begins with a product name has skipped the part that determines the outcome.

## Scouting and assessment

Walk the field in a W pattern and inspect the underside of leaves at three heights on each plant, because most foliar pathogens establish on shaded lower foliage before they are visible from the path. Record the proportion of plants showing symptoms rather than a general impression, and note whether affected plants are clustered or scattered. Clustered symptoms indicate a spreading focus and justify treating a buffer around the cluster; scattered single plants across a whole field more often indicate seed-borne or systemic infection, which a foliar spray will not correct.

## Sanitation

Sanitation is the first control step. Infected foliage and fruit should be removed from the field and destroyed by burning or deep burial; leaving residue on field margins or in compost heaps allows the pathogen to survive and re-infect. Sanitation should be completed before fungicide application so that treated surfaces are not immediately recontaminated. Tools and hands should be cleaned between the affected block and clean blocks, and workers should move from clean rows to infected rows rather than the reverse.

## Protectant fungicides

Protectant fungicides based on mancozeb are applied at 2.0 to 2.5 grams per litre of water and act on the leaf surface, so complete coverage of both leaf surfaces is essential. Treat the affected zone plus a buffer of at least two rows, as neighbouring plants are usually already exposed even when they show no symptoms. A protectant cannot cure tissue that is already infected; its value is in stopping new infections, which is why it is applied early and repeated rather than applied heavily once. Copper oxychloride 50 percent WP at 3.0 grams per litre is an alternative protectant where a copper-tolerant programme is wanted.

## Cultural practice and prevention

On a field with no symptoms, preventive practice is the whole programme and no spray is warranted. Rotate solanaceous crops with cereals or legumes on a minimum two-year cycle, stake and prune to open the canopy, avoid overhead irrigation in the evening, and remove volunteer plants and solanaceous weeds from field margins where pathogens overwinter. Certified disease-free seed and resistant varieties remove more risk than any spray programme adds.

## Records

Keep a dated record of every application: product, active ingredient, concentration, area covered, and the weather that day. This is what makes the next season's decisions better than this season's, and it is also what a resistance-management rotation depends on, since a rotation cannot be planned without knowing what was used and when.
