# Tomato Septoria Leaf Spot and Leaf Mold

## Septoria leaf spot — identification

Septoria lycopersici produces numerous small circular spots, 2 to 4 millimetres across, with a pale grey or tan centre and a dark brown margin. The distinguishing feature is the tiny black fruiting bodies visible in the centre of an older lesion under a hand lens. Spots are far more numerous and much smaller than early blight lesions and carry no concentric rings. Infection begins on the lowest leaves after fruit set and moves upward, and heavy infection defoliates a plant from the base within two to three weeks.

## Leaf mold — identification

Leaf mold, caused by Passalora fulva, shows as pale green to yellow patches on the upper leaf surface with no defined margin, and an olive-green to grey-brown velvety growth on the corresponding underside. It is overwhelmingly a protected-cultivation disease: greenhouse and polyhouse tomatoes with high humidity and poor air movement are where it appears, and it is uncommon in open field crops with free air movement. Turning a leaf over is diagnostic, since nothing else on tomato produces that velvet underside.

## Conditions and cultural response

Both diseases require extended leaf wetness. Septoria is driven by rain splash and overhead irrigation at 20 to 25 degrees Celsius; leaf mold needs relative humidity above 85 percent and is halted by ventilation more reliably than by any spray. For leaf mold, increasing air movement, reducing plant density and venting at night to prevent condensation on the canopy are the primary controls, and a spray programme applied without correcting humidity will not hold. For septoria, mulching and removing the lowest infected leaves reduces the inoculum reaching new foliage.

## Chemical control

For septoria leaf spot, apply mancozeb 75 percent WP at 2.0 to 2.5 grams per litre or chlorothalonil 75 percent WP at 2.0 grams per litre at first symptom, repeated at 7 to 10 day intervals. For leaf mold, chlorothalonil 75 percent WP at 2.0 grams per litre is the protectant of choice, and difenoconazole 25 percent EC at 0.5 millilitres per litre may be used where infection is established, to a maximum of two applications per season. Coverage of the leaf underside is essential for both, and is the usual reason a leaf mold programme fails.

## Follow-up

Re-inspect after 7 days, counting the highest tier of foliage showing new lesions rather than the total number of spots, since the height of the infection front is what predicts defoliation. Remove and destroy stripped lower leaves rather than leaving them on the ground, as both pathogens survive on debris and on infected stakes and trellis wire, which should be disinfected between seasons.
