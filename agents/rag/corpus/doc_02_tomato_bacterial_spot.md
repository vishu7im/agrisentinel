# Tomato Bacterial Spot — Identification and Management

## Identification

Bacterial spot is caused by Xanthomonas species and produces small, dark, water-soaked spots on leaves that later dry to an angular brown lesion with a narrow yellow border. On fruit the spots are raised and scabby, which is what makes the disease a marketability problem rather than only a yield problem. Held against the light, young lesions look greasy and translucent — the feature that separates bacterial spot from the dry, ringed lesions of early blight, which is the misidentification that most often leads to a wasted fungicide programme.

## Conditions that drive an outbreak

The bacterium spreads in water: rain splash, overhead irrigation, and handling of wet foliage during staking or pruning. Warm and wet weather above 24 degrees Celsius with sustained leaf wetness is the driver. It is frequently seed-borne and seedling-borne, so an outbreak that appears simultaneously across an entire field, rather than spreading from a focus, points to infected transplants rather than field spread.

## First response when infection is confirmed

Stop all handling of the crop while foliage is wet, and stop overhead irrigation immediately. Do not remove large amounts of foliage: unlike fungal diseases, aggressive pruning of a bacterial infection creates wounds that are direct entry points and typically accelerates spread. Remove only severely affected plants, whole, and destroy them away from the field.

## Chemical control

Bacterial spot cannot be cured by any product available to a grower, and fungicides intended for fungal foliar disease have no effect on it. Copper oxychloride 50 percent WP at 3.0 grams per litre suppresses further spread by protecting uninfected tissue, applied at 7 day intervals while conditions remain wet, and is more effective when tank-mixed with mancozeb 75 percent WP at 2.0 grams per litre. Expect suppression, not elimination, and set the grower's expectations accordingly.

## Prevention for the following season

Use certified disease-free seed, or treat seed in hot water at 50 degrees Celsius for 25 minutes before sowing. Rotate away from solanaceous crops for two years, since the bacterium survives on infected debris. Raise transplants away from production fields, and discard any tray showing symptoms rather than transplanting from it, because a single infected tray can seed an entire field.
